The problem

Snappfood is the largest food delivery platform in its market, and it runs on its vendors. When vendors are unhappy, they leave.

The company already collected complaints. It had a ticketing system, a social media presence, and a complaints channel inside the vendor-side app. What it did not have was any way to look at that data as a whole. Nobody could answer which problem was the most common, whether a given problem was getting better or worse, or which team's backlog was actually hurting vendors most. Tickets went to the responsible team and disappeared into it.

The failure mode was visible from the outside: vendors would call and say they had filed the same ticket several times and nothing had changed. Individually, every ticket had been routed correctly. Collectively, nobody was watching.

For that cycle, the Commercial Planning team was given a single mission — raise vendor satisfaction. You cannot raise a number you are not measuring, so the first problem was measurement.

The scale

To get a direct signal, five promoters began calling vendors: roughly 120 calls each per day, around 6,000 calls a month, each two to seven minutes long. About 500 hours of Persian-language audio every month.

That volume is the whole problem. Five hundred hours is far past what any team can listen to, and listening is only step one — someone then has to decide what each call was actually about and count it consistently against every other call. Done by hand, the analysis would have finished long after the cycle it was meant to inform.

What made it hard

  • Persian is a low-resource language for speech models. Transcription quality that would be unremarkable in English is genuinely difficult here, and this shaped every downstream decision.
  • The recording conditions were the worst case. Vendors answered from restaurant floors and working kitchens. Background noise was the norm, not the exception.
  • Vendors did not state problems cleanly. People describe frustration, not categories. A complaint arrived mid-sentence, wrapped in context, often with several separate issues in one call.
  • Attribution was frequently wrong. A vendor would report a problem under the team they associated with the symptom rather than the team that owned the cause.
  • No labelled data existed. Nothing to fine-tune on, nothing to validate against, no prior taxonomy.

Approach

The obvious design is one model, one prompt: audio in, structured complaints out. I tested that and rejected it. Asking a single model to transcribe noisy Persian audio, separate speakers, identify multiple distinct complaints, normalise colloquial speech, and classify each one pushes every weakness at once, and when the output is wrong there is no way to tell which part failed.

Splitting the work into stages meant each stage could be evaluated on its own, each stage could use whichever provider was actually best at that specific task, and later stages could correct errors introduced by earlier ones. A messy transcription is recoverable if a subsequent stage is explicitly responsible for cleaning it. Inside one prompt, it is not.

Provider selection was benchmarked, not assumed. I built a fixed sample of around 50 calls and ran it through each candidate at each stage — hosted models from multiple providers alongside locally-hosted open models — then selected per stage on measured performance rather than picking one vendor for the whole pipeline. The models that won differed by stage, which is precisely why routing per stage was worth the added complexity.

Splitting a call into complaint units before extraction matters more than it looks. It is what makes the output countable: one call with three problems becomes three rows, not one blurred summary, so the same call can register against three different owning teams.

Data model

The pipeline fed a schema I designed from scratch: one table per complaint source — ticketing system, social media, promoter calls, in-app vendor reports — plus a unified table consolidating all four into a common structure, plus metadata tables.

The unification is the point. A complaint raised by phone and the same complaint raised through the app previously lived in unrelated systems and were never counted together. Bringing them into one structure was what turned four disconnected streams into a single measurable signal.

What I rejected, and why

  • One model for the entire pipeline — worse results and higher cost than routing per stage.
  • End-to-end in a single prompt — not feasible; model limitations surfaced immediately and failures were undiagnosable.
  • Fine-tuning a dedicated model — no labelled Persian data existed, and building a corpus would have consumed the cycle the project was meant to inform.
  • Classical NLP — keyword and rule-based approaches could not handle unstructured conversational Persian with implicit, mis-attributed complaints.

Failure handling

Unusable audio was tagged as such rather than silently dropped, as were segments too unclear to interpret — so the gap stayed visible in the data instead of quietly biasing it. Calls containing no complaint were a normal outcome, not an error, and were recorded as satisfied.

Validation and operation

I manually spot-checked over 100 calls end to end against pipeline output. This is not a formal evaluation set — there was no ground truth to build one from — and I would describe it as exactly what it was: enough to establish that the extraction was reliable, not enough to put a precision figure on it.

The pipeline was written as parameterised stages and run on a weekly cadence.

Outcome

Complaint processing time dropped by roughly 80% against the manual baseline — an estimate from the team lead comparing the new process to what the work had previously required, not an instrumented measurement.

The more important change was categorical rather than incremental. The business could see vendor satisfaction as a continuous, decomposed signal for the first time. Complaint volume by issue, by owning team, and by month became something you could look up rather than something you had to guess at, and fix prioritisation moved onto that basis.

What surprised me was how responsive the signal turned out to be. When the business acted on a problem area, the complaint data moved in the expected direction within months. That is the outcome you hope for and still do not expect to see so clearly — it meant the pipeline was not just producing plausible output, it was tracking reality closely enough to function as a feedback loop.

The system was handed over to my successor when I left.

What I would do differently

I designed the full schema — all four sources, unified table, metadata — before any of it had processed real data. It worked, but it was a bet.

I would build for one source first, run it end to end, break it, fix it, and only then generalise the structure to the rest. The cost of finding a modelling mistake after four sources are built on top of it is far higher than the cost of building the fourth source twice.

Part One — Forecasting competitor prices

The problem

The company sells steel through the national commodity exchange. On offer day, every supplier announces the price at which they will sell. Buyers then choose.

That makes pricing a bet with a narrow window. Price too high and competitors take the trade. Price too low and you win the trade and lose the margin. The planning team had to make that call for each offer, and what would have made it easy — knowing what everyone else was about to announce — was exactly what nobody knew.

So the question was direct: what price will each competing supplier announce this week?

Why the existing model wasn't used

A forecasting model already existed. It was deployed, it was monitored, and it carried roughly 38% mean absolute error.

At that error level, a forecast is not a weak signal — it is noise wearing the costume of information. It was accurate enough to be usable for a handful of the most predictable suppliers and useless for the rest, which meant it could not be trusted as a system. The planning team did the rational thing and ignored it, pricing on experience and instinct instead.

This is the part worth sitting with. Rebuilding it meant not just improving accuracy but clearing the accumulated evidence that this kind of thing does not work here.

Approach

I built the model on four years of national exchange history: demand, traded quantities, the set of participating companies, and time-based features, enriched with foreign exchange rates — because in an import-exposed market, currency movement drives input costs and therefore pricing behaviour.

The final model combined XGBoost and ARIMA, because they failed in different places. Neither was better everywhere, so choosing one meant accepting known weakness across half the forecast range.

What did not work: LSTM and plain regression. The LSTM is the interesting failure — a deep sequence model on four years of weekly observations is a model with far more capacity than the data can constrain. It fit and it did not generalise. Regression failed in the opposite direction, too rigid for a series with this much nonlinear interaction between demand, participation, and currency.

Outcome

Mean absolute error fell from roughly 38% to under 20% — a reduction of 18.5 percentage points against the legacy model on the same evaluation.

More usefully: the forecast crossed the line from noise into something the planning and supply teams actually priced against. The model ran in Python against the company database on a weekly cycle — once suppliers announced their base prices, the feature set completed and the forecast generated automatically. A failure surfaced as a failed database job rather than a silently stale number, which matters when someone is about to make a pricing decision on the output.

Part Two — A credit scoring system

The problem

The company extended trade credit to its customers. The decision of who received it, and how much, was made by the CEO, based on his own knowledge of each customer and how they had behaved historically.

To be fair to that process: it was not arbitrary and it was not usually wrong. He knew these customers. But it did not scale, it could not be delegated, it could not be explained to a customer who was refused, and it left with the person holding it. It was judgement, and judgement is not a system.

Around 300 customers, and roughly $1M of credit exposure the business could absorb.

Approach

I structured the scoring around the 5C credit framework — character, capacity, capital, collateral, conditions — rather than inventing a scoring scheme from scratch. That choice was as much about adoption as about modelling. A finance team can interrogate a framework they already recognise, and a model nobody in finance can argue with is a model nobody in finance will use.

Inputs came from two directions: financial reports and actual payment behaviour, alongside firmographic signals — production capacity, assets, company size.

The tiers were not a modelling output. The model produced a continuous score; the six tiers, from full credit eligibility down to no credit, were drawn by the business. Where the cut points fall is a question about risk appetite, and risk appetite belongs to the people carrying the risk.

Earning trust

The validation that mattered was not the technical one, though historical data and known defaults gave us that.

It was this: we scored the existing customer base and showed that the customers who had held credit for years, on the CEO's own judgement, scored highly under the model. The system agreed with the expert where the expert was confident.

That reframed the entire conversation. The model was no longer a machine proposing to overrule a person — it was a formalisation of reasoning the business already trusted, extended consistently to customers nobody had time to assess that carefully. The disagreements then became worth examining rather than worth resisting.

The pipeline ran monthly against fresh data from the finance team, replacing an opaque process with a documented and repeatable one.

What I would do differently

I would use fewer features. The firmographic signals — size, capacity, assets — carried far less weight than actual financial behaviour, and in hindsight they mostly added surface area. How a customer has paid is a sharper predictor of how they will pay than how large they are. A leaner model would have been easier to explain, easier to maintain, and probably no less accurate.

Day one

A commodity trading company. Vessels arrive, goods clear, contracts settle, money moves.

What existed as "data" on my first day: a PDF, delivered every two weeks, reporting national import figures for the commodity. And a set of Excel files holding the company's own trading history.

That was the analytics function. Not a bad one — the actual one. Everything else lived in the heads of the people doing the work, and in documents that had to be read one at a time by someone who already knew what they were looking for.

I was hired as the only data person, which meant the first question was not what should I build but what should I build first.

Sequencing

With no data function at all, everything is urgent and nothing is prioritised for you. I worked in four stages, and the order was the decision:

  • Find out what each source actually contains. Not what it is called or what people believe it holds — what is genuinely in it, field by field. This is unglamorous and it is where most of the surprises are.
  • Clean each dataset. Establish what a valid record looks like before anything downstream depends on it.
  • Automate the manual steps that feed the data. This came before the dashboards, deliberately. Removing the manual step removes the error class, and it is much cheaper to do before people have started trusting the output.
  • Then model, load, and visualise. Only once the inputs were reliable.

The instinct is to build the dashboard first, because it is the visible thing and it is what leadership asked for. The dashboard is also worthless if the numbers underneath it are wrong, and worse than worthless once people have begun making decisions on it.

Modelling the domain

The schema grew to over ten tables in PostgreSQL, built around the entities the business actually runs on: vessels, vendors, goods, contracts, the shipments belonging to each contract, and the trips a vessel makes.

The hard part was not the entities. It was the places where the real world refused to be one-to-one. A contract can have several consignees. A contract can be denominated in several currencies. Every one of these is obvious to a trader and invisible in a naive schema — and each one, modelled wrongly, produces numbers that look plausible and are quietly incorrect.

Getting these right was most of the modelling work, and finding them meant asking the operations team about cases they considered too routine to mention.

Data reaches the system three ways: parsed from documents, pulled from national trade and customs reporting, and entered by hand where no other source exists. Reducing that last category is ongoing.

What people use

Python ETL pipelines feed four Metabase dashboards, used by a twenty-person operations team for vessel tracking, trip-level analytics, and daily trading reports.

Two of the dashboards are the core: one covering the company's own position, one covering the wider national market it competes in. The other two were built on external sources that have since become unavailable — which is its own lesson. A dashboard is only as durable as the data feeding it, and when you do not own the source, you are one policy change away from losing it. Anything built on external data now gets designed with that assumption.

I chose Metabase over Power BI or Superset for a reason that had nothing to do with features: the learning curve is short. I am the only data person here, and I will not always be. A tool that the next person can pick up without training is worth more to this company than one with a longer capability ceiling that leaves with me.

Defining what to measure

Some of the most useful work was not technical. The company was operating with metrics that had never been formally defined — mean sales price, mean purchase price, and P&L did not exist as agreed, computed numbers until they were specified and built.

Naming a metric sounds trivial and is not. It forces the questions nobody had needed to answer: which trades count, at what point a price is fixed, how currency is handled when a contract spans several. Answering those turned informal understanding into something the whole team could reference.

A related example: leadership asked for "a platform where we can see all the invoices, contracts, and performance in one place." That is one sentence describing two different systems. I split it — the analytical half is a dashboard layer, the operational half is an ERP — and made the case that conflating them produces a tool that does neither job. The ERP is now under development.

Being the only one

The technical challenge of working alone is manageable. The part I miss is the other thing a team gives you: three people arguing toward a solution none of them would have reached individually. Working solo, you get your own first idea, and you have to be disciplined about interrogating it the way a colleague would.

Where it stands

The foundation is in place and in daily use. The ERP is being built. The share of data still entering by hand is the number I most want to reduce.

Research

Detecting cognitive load in EEG using heterogeneous graph neural networks

M.S. thesis, Tarbiat Modares University · Submitted to Scientific Reports · Read the preprint →

The idea. Most EEG models treat the signal as a flat vector or a time series. Both throw away something real: the brain is a network, and cognitive load shows up in how regions relate to each other, not only in what each electrode records on its own. A graph neural network can represent that relational structure directly.

Method

Two public datasets harmonised and pooled to force exposure to hardware and subject variation, segmented into 30-second hyper-windows. Two representations: a channel graph encoding inter-electrode connectivity, and a heterogeneous graph encoding the three relation types. Four graph architectures (GCN, GAT, RGCN, heterogeneous GAT) evaluated against an LSTM baseline plus convolutional, transformer, and classical baselines, under a strict subject-independent 5-fold protocol.

Results

The heterogeneous RGCN performed best among the graph models — accuracy 0.760, ROC-AUC 0.836 — significantly outperforming the LSTM baseline. An edge-type ablation confirmed the three relation types contribute complementary information rather than redundant signal.

The result I did not want

A random forest on the same handcrafted features matched the RGCN on accuracy and beat it on ROC-AUC. That is worth stating plainly: on this data, at this scale, the graph architecture did not earn its complexity. The relational structure is real and the ablation shows it carries information — but a well-chosen classical model remained competitive, and any honest reading of the work has to say so.

Fairness

A sex-based subgroup analysis found comparable accuracy (male 0.761, female 0.716) but a true-positive-rate gap of 0.131 — a sizeable disparity in who the model actually detects, invisible if you only report accuracy. It is a small illustration of why recall-based fairness metrics matter.
